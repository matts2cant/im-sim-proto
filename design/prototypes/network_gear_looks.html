Linking bay

A floor-standing open rack that holds the network gear of an area, with its cables bundled up to the ceiling. Furniture, not a device: the units inside are the devices.

60 × 190 cm, 40 rack units

Switch: large / small

Large: a 1-unit rack switch, one long row of 24 sockets. Small: a flat desktop box with 5 sockets. Both: a port light per cabled socket.

large 48 × 4.5 cm (1U) · small 17 × 3.5 cm

Router: large / small

Large: a 2-unit rack router with a fan grille and few ports; the uplink port is ringed orange. Small: a desktop box, wedge-shaped, with the same orange uplink.

large 48 × 9 cm (2U) · small 22 × 4.5 cm

Servers

Rack units with drive bays (the Map's server icon). A mail server carries an envelope on its front; other servers do not.

48 × 13 cm (3U) or 9 cm (2U)

What tells them apart in a bay, strongest first: the height of the unit (1U switch, 2U router, 3U server), the front (a long socket row, a fan grille with an orange uplink, drive bays), then the colour band on the left ear and the Map icon. Each unit keeps one status light and a name label on its front.

Rules: no "E: …" prompt any more. Within reach (2 m) the brackets are full white: E acts on the framed thing. Beyond reach, up to 8 m, the brackets are dimmed: the frame only names the thing and shows its status. Walls block it. What the prompt used to warn about ("Too heavy", "Locked: keycard", "No room to put down") shows as a short message when you press E and it fails. The status square uses the status lights' colours; cut off blinks like the light does. A door is not a network device: its controller on the wall beside it is (keypad, card reader or control box), and that is where the light, the cable and the status are. Wall jacks, panic buttons and lasers lose their light and show their status here only.
